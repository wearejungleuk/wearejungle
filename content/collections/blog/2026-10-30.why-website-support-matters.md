  -
    type: paragraph

        type: text
        text: 'A typical small business WordPress site has somewhere between 20 and 30 plugins installed. Each one is a piece of code written by somebody you have never met, doing a specific job on your site. They are brilliant when they work. They are the single biggest source of security problems on the web when they are not kept current.'

        text: 'Speed matters more than most people realise too. The same report found the most targeted of those flaws were being exploited within five hours of going public, and about half of the high-impact ones were being hit within 24. "I will get round to it next month" is not a plan. "I will do it after the weekend" is often too slow.'

    type: paragraph

        type: text
        text: 'Here is the one that catches people out. Forty-six percent of those vulnerabilities had no fix available from the plugin developer at the point they went public. Which means pressing update does nothing. In those cases the only sensible move is to replace the plugin with a maintained alternative, or remove it altogether. That is a judgement call, not a button, and it is why "press update when WordPress tells you to" is not quite the same job as proper plugin maintenance.'

        text: 'Most people avoid updates because updates can break things. Fair enough, they can. The way around that is to back the site up first, apply the update on a copy, check nothing has fallen over and only then push it to the live site. That is a lot more fiddly to do than it is to describe. It is also most of our Thursdays.'

        text: 'Updating the old code underneath'
  -
    type: paragraph
    content:
      -
        type: text
        text: 'PHP does not just get patched. It changes. Every new version removes functions that have been retired and tweaks the way some things work. Mostly for the better. The catch is that older themes, custom code and abandoned plugins often lean on exactly the pieces that have been taken out. So when the hosting is upgraded, those older bits stop working. Sometimes a page starts behaving oddly. Sometimes it goes blank white.'
  -
    type: paragraph
    content:
      -
        type: text
        text: 'This is why a lot of sites end up stuck on old PHP. The owner, or whoever built the site originally, puts the upgrade off because the site might break. Then another year goes by. Then PHP 7 goes end of life. Then another year. By the time the upgrade genuinely has to happen, the gap between where the site is and where the server needs to be is enormous, and the job of closing it is a proper piece of work rather than a routine update. Left too long, the cheapest sensible option can quietly become a rebuild rather than a repair.'
  -
    type: paragraph
    content:
      -
        type: text
        text: 'The way we handle it is unglamorous and effective. Check the site code against the version of PHP we are moving it to. Find anything that will break and fix it or replace it. Spin up a copy of the whole site on the newer version, test the bits that actually matter: contact forms, checkout, logins, the admin itself. Only switch the live site over once the copy is behaving. The client sees a quiet hop from one version to the next. We see the twenty hours underneath.'
