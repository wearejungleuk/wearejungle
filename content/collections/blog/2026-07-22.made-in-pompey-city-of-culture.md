So when I heard Portsmouth was bidding for UK City of Culture 2029, something stirred. Not the polished excitement you'd expect from a press release, but something more instinctive. The kind of feeling you get walking down Albert Road on a Friday night, hearing music spill out of a doorway, or spotting a bit of guerrilla street art tucked down a side street that wasn't there last week."

It's the bands playing to thirty people who'd die for them. It's the ravers, the punks, the record shop diehards and the graffiti writers. It's the city's refusal to be anything other than exactly what it is."

        type: text
        text: " is probably the city's best known artist and for good reason. You'll know the work when you see it - those enormous reflective eyes staring out from the sides of buildings, the painted tin cans, the little everyman figures tucked into corners you'd walk past without a second thought. Free Art Friday started right here in Portsmouth - the idea being that you'd head out on a Friday morning not knowing what you might find. A painting on a bench, something propped against a lamppost with a note saying take it home. It's now hidden art in 20 countries across the world. That's a Portsmouth idea that went global, and most people outside this city have no idea it came from here. The whole thing started with someone pretending to go for a jog in the evenings, picking up bits of rubbish off the streets of Portsmouth, spending the week working on them, and leaving them on windowsills on the way to a teaching job on a Friday morning. That's as DIY as it gets. His studio is above the Wedgewood Rooms, which tells you something about how tightly this city's creative community is woven together."
